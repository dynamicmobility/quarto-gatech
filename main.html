Muscle Synergy Priors Enhance Biomechanical Fidelity in Predictive Musculoskeletal Locomotion Simulation

Literature Review, FACTS Lab + Dynamo Lab

Ayah Ahmad

Georgia Institute of Technology

October 29, 2026

Motivation: Musculoskeletal Modeling

  • Musculoskeletal models (MSMs) combine skeletal anatomy, muscle–tendon dynamics, and neural control in a physics simulator
  • Goal: a mechanistic account of locomotion, i.e. why a gait emerges, not just what it looks like
    • How does coordination fail after stroke? How should an exoskeleton assist?
  • The challenge: the human motor system is massively redundant
    • The model used here has 21 DoF but 90 muscles
    • Many muscle-activation patterns produce the same joint motion

This paper [1]: constrain an RL controller to a low-dimensional muscle synergy action space and ask whether the resulting gait is more biomechanically human-like, not just kinematically.

Frame the talk: the question is fidelity at the level of kinetics and muscle activity, not whether the model can walk.

[1]
I. Park, E. Choi, J. Ahn, and J. Ahn, “Muscle synergy priors enhance biomechanical fidelity in predictive musculoskeletal locomotion simulation,” arXiv, 2026.

Background

Learning Locomotion with Musculoskeletal Models

Motion imitation [2], [3]

  • Policy outputs muscle excitations
  • Reward: track a pre-recorded mocap clip
  • Reproduces reference kinematics well

Predictive simulation [4], [5], [6]

  • No reference trajectory in the reward
  • Bio-plausible objectives (effort, stability) and curricula
  • Gait emerges from the task

Problems with imitation:

  • Kinematically feasible \(\neq\) muscularly plausible: muscles are controlled independently, so the policy can use conflicting activations (e.g. co-contraction inside a muscle group) that no human would use
  • Joint kinetics and muscle coordination stay underdetermined
  • Brittle outside the demonstration distribution (new speeds, slopes)

The gap in predictive simulation: motion looks plausible, but quantitative agreement with human kinetics and EMG across speeds and slopes is still poor.

Muscle Synergies

  • Evidence that the CNS doesn’t control muscles one at a time: it recruits groups of muscles that activate together with fixed relative weights [7], [8]
  • A small number of synergies (≈ 4–6 per leg in walking EMG) reproduces complex movement
  • Synergy structure is clinically meaningful: after stroke, the paretic limb shows fewer, merged modules [9]

Definition 1: Muscle synergy (spatial)

A fixed, non-negative weighting \(\mathbf{h}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{m}_{\ge 0}\) over \(m\) muscles. The activations are a time-varying combination of \(k \ll m\) synergies: \[ \mathbf{a}(t) \approx \sum_{j=1}^{k} w_j(t)\,\mathbf{h}_j, \qquad w_j(t) \ge 0 \]

  • Synergy action spaces already help sample efficiency in over-actuated RL [10], [11]. The open question: do they give more human-like biomechanics?

Method

Method Framework

1. Experiment

1 subject, 10 overground walking strides

Mocap + force plates

2. Inverse analysis

OpenSim IK + MocoInverse

\(\rightarrow\) estimated activations of 40 muscles

3. Synergy extraction

NMF: \(M \approx WH\)

Keep \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times 40}\)

4. Predictive RL

SAC policy outputs synergy weights

Hyfydy, H2190 model

  • Human data is used only to build the action space, never as a tracking target
  • Baseline: the same pipeline with an independent controller that sets every muscle excitation directly
  • Evaluated against two external treadmill datasets (22 + 8 subjects) [12], [13]

J. Camargo, A. Ramanathan, W. Flanagan, and A. Young, “A comprehensive, open-source dataset of lower limb biomechanics in multiple conditions of stairs, ramps, and level-ground ambulation and transitions,” Journal of Biomechanics, 2021.
[13]
K. Scherpereel, D. Molinaro, O. Inan, M. Shepherd, and A. Young, “A human lower-limb biomechanics and wearable sensors dataset during cyclic and non-cyclic activities,” Scientific Data, 2023.

Data Collection

  • One healthy young male participant (28 y, 69.6 kg, 176 cm)
  • 10 trials of 10 m overground walking; one stride kept per trial
  • Optical motion capture: 12 OptiTrack cameras at 100 Hz, 38 reflective markers
  • 5 force plates at 1000 Hz (4 AMTI, 1 Bertec)
  • Preprocessing
    • 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter, 12 Hz cutoff
    • Heel strike = vertical GRF first exceeds 15 N

Note

No EMG is recorded. Muscle activity is estimated from kinematics and GRFs (next slide), so the synergies come from an optimization, not measured muscle signals.

Identifying Muscle Synergies from Data

Muscle activity estimation

  • Model: H2190, 3D, 21 DoF, 90 muscles (incl. lumbar joint and 10 trunk muscles), ported from Hyfydy to OpenSim
  • Scale the model to the subject, then inverse kinematics for joint angles
  • MocoInverse [14]: minimize \(\sum u_i^2\) (plus reserve/residual effort) subject to the measured kinematics and GRFs

Synergy acquisition: non-negative matrix factorization on the right-leg activations

\[ \underbrace{M}_{T \times 40} \;\approx\; \underbrace{W}_{T \times k}\;\underbrace{H}_{k \times 40}, \qquad W, H \ge 0, \quad k = 10 \]

  • \(W\): temporal activation coefficients. They are discarded; the policy produces them online
  • \(H\): spatial synergy weights, the fixed prior embedded in the controller

\(k = 10\) is chosen without a reported VAF criterion, and is high compared with the 4–6 modules typical of EMG studies.

[14]
C. L. Dembia, N. A. Bianco, A. Falisse, J. L. Hicks, and S. L. Delp, “OpenSim moco: Musculoskeletal optimal control,” PLOS Computational Biology, 2020.

Training a Policy with Reinforcement Learning

Action space (30-D)

  • 10 synergy weights, right leg
  • 10 synergy weights, left leg
  • 10 independent torso/pelvis activations
  • Leg muscles: \(\mathbf{a}_{\text{leg}} = H^\top \mathbf{s}_{\text{leg}} \in \mathbb{R}^{40}\)
  • Phase-based left/right mirroring at heel strike

Observations

  • Muscle fiber lengths/velocities, forces, excitations, activations
  • Joint angles/velocities, head orientation and angular velocity
  • Feet relative to pelvis, GRFs, COM velocity, target speed

Environment: Hyfydy [15]

  • 40 Hz control, 1000-step (25 s) episodes
  • Speed curriculum: \(0.7 \to 1.6 \to 0.7\) m/s
  • 10,000 random tile terrains, slopes \(\pm 6^\circ\)

Learner: SAC [16], Stable-Baselines3

  • 75M steps per seed, 5 seeds, RTX 4090
  • MLP policy (512-512-256), buffer \(3 \times 10^6\)
  • Best seed by return kept for analysis

T. Geijtenbeek, “The hyfydy simulation software.” https://hyfydy.com, 2021.

Reward Design

\[ R = w_{\text{vel}} R_{\text{vel}} + w_{\text{effort}} R_{\text{effort}} + w_{\text{rom}} R_{\text{rom}} + w_{\text{fall}} R_{\text{fall}} \]

  • Velocity: \(R_{\text{vel}} = R_{\text{AP}}\, R_{\text{ML}}\, R_{\text{head}}\)
    • \(R_{\text{AP}}\): plateaued Gaussian around \(v_{\text{target}}\) (\(\pm 0.05\) m/s dead band)
    • \(R_{\text{ML}}\): Gaussian penalty on lateral drift
    • \(R_{\text{head}}\): Gaussian penalty on head angular velocity, per axis
  • Effort: \(R_{\text{effort}} = \sum_i a_i^2\) (negative weight)
  • Range of motion: penalize knee hyperextension and lumbar outside \([-20^\circ, 10^\circ]\)
  • Fall: termination penalty

There is no imitation term. Only a target speed and generic biomechanical plausibility terms are used, so any human-likeness comes from the action space.

Evaluation Protocol

  • 10 rollouts per controller, last 10 strides of each, normalized to 0–100% gait cycle
  • Conditions matched to open-source treadmill datasets
    • Speed: level walking, 0.7–1.8 m/s, 22 subjects [12]
    • Slope: \(\pm 5^\circ\) at 1.2 m/s, 8 subjects [13]
  • Compared: sagittal joint angles, joint moments, GRFs (AP + vertical), and EMG for 8 lower-limb muscles

Definition 2: RMSE ratio

\[ \text{RMSE ratio} = \frac{\text{RMSE}(\text{sim}, \text{human})} {\overline{\text{RMSE}}(\text{human}_i, \text{human}_j)} \] A ratio near 1.0 means the simulation is as close to the data as one person is to another.

  • Also: Pearson \(r\) between waveforms, against the cross-human distribution of \(r\)

Results

Reinforcement Learning Performance

  • Both controllers train stably on uneven terrain with the same curriculum
  • The independent controller learns faster and reaches a higher return
  • A higher return here doesn’t mean a more human-like gait (next slides)

Similarity of Muscle Activities

  • 1.2 m/s level walking, 8 muscles vs. EMG envelopes of 22 subjects
  • Independent: correlations fall outside cross-human variability for 5 / 8 muscles; often negative (GAS, ST, BF)
  • Synergistic: within the human envelope for all 8
    • Plantarflexors (SOL, GAS) match timing and magnitude
    • ST, BF, RF are weaker, but these muscles also vary most between humans
  • Caveat: TA correlation for the synergistic controller is still low (\(r \approx 0.2\))

Kinematic and Kinetic Similarity: Speed

  • Both controllers walk stably from 0.7 to 1.8 m/s, past the 1.6 m/s maximum of the training curriculum
  • Human data shows monotonic speed modulation in specific gait phases
  • Synergistic keeps these trends (red arrows)
  • Independent reverses or flattens them (green arrows):
    • Hip angle in swing
    • Knee angle in loading response and swing
    • Ankle moment in loading response

Kinematic and Kinetic Similarity: Slope

  • Uphill (\(+5^\circ\)), level, and downhill (\(-5^\circ\)) at 1.2 m/s
  • Both controllers reproduce the basic GRF structure
    • AP: braking then propulsion
    • Vertical: double peak
  • The largest independent-controller error is the knee moment in loading response, RMSE ratio 8.56, about \(2\times\) the synergistic controller’s worst moment error

Quantitative Summary: RMSE Ratios

Ratio > 3.0: 23 independent vs. 7 synergistic

Ratio > 5.0: 7 independent vs. 0 synergistic

Synergistic worst case: hip moment in loading response (4.16 / 4.54)

  • The synergistic controller trades a larger global hip-angle offset for far fewer extreme knee, ankle, and GRF errors

Limitations and Critical Notes

Stated by the authors

  • Level walking and modest slopes only, so no running, turning, or stairs
  • Synergies come from inverse simulation, not EMG
  • Fixed synergy set and \(k\) across all speeds and slopes
  • Single participant used to build \(H\)

Points for discussion

  • The independent baseline has a 90-D action space and the synergistic controller 30-D; is the gain from physiology or just from lower dimensionality? A random or PCA basis ablation is missing
  • Only the best of 5 seeds is analyzed, so variability across seeds isn’t reported
  • MocoInverse minimizes squared activation, so \(H\) partly carries that optimization prior and not only a neural one
  • The reward is lower for the synergistic controller, so the fidelity gain has a task-performance cost

Open Questions

  1. How can we leverage data from multiple subjects?
  2. How do we determine the optimal \(k\)?
  3. Could the muscle synergy matrix be time-varying (over some window)?
  4. Would a patient-specific (e.g. post-stroke, merged-module) \(H\) reproduce pathological gait [9]?
  5. Does the prior transfer to assistive-device control loops (exoskeleton co-simulation)?
[9]
D. J. Clark, L. H. Ting, F. E. Zajac, R. R. Neptune, and S. A. Kautz, “Merging of healthy motor modules predicts reduced locomotor performance and muscle coordination complexity post-stroke,” Journal of Neurophysiology, 2010.

Conclusion

Takeaways

  • Using muscle synergies as the action space is a cheap, physiology-informed prior: 10 strides from one person are enough
  • Without any imitation reward, it gives
    • EMG timing within human variability for all 8 muscles tested
    • Speed- and slope-dependent trends in the same direction as human data
    • No extreme (RMSE ratio > 5) kinematic or kinetic deviations
  • Lower RL return but higher biomechanical fidelity: the reward isn’t a good proxy for human-likeness
  • Next step: a better structure for control, not only better objectives
