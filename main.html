Motivation

Why learn locomotion policies for musculoskeletal models (MSMs)?

  • Enables a simulation-based study of human gait, and how it physiologically changes due to pathology.
  • Easier data collection for training and personalizing assistive device controllers.

Assistive devices, like exoskeletons, can significantly increase functional mobility, especially in older age [1].

The paper starts from the clinical side: locomotion underpins independence, and declines in mobility are associated with injury, hospitalization, and reduced well-being.

Their argument is that understanding locomotion needs a mechanistic account — not just describing how people walk, but explaining how the neuromuscular system produces movement, and how that fails, adapts, or reorganizes with pathology, training, or assistive interventions. Musculoskeletal simulation is a good framework for that because it puts skeletal anatomy, muscle and tendon dynamics, and neural control into one physics-based model.

That’s also why it matters for assistive devices: if the simulation is physiologically realistic, it can be used to design and evaluate exoskeleton controllers — the paper names this as a direction in its discussion and conclusion.

[1]
M. Grimmer, R. Riener, C. J. Walsh, and A. Seyfarth, “Mobility related physical and functional losses due to aging and disease - a motivation for lower limb exoskeletons,” Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation, Jan. 2019.

Background

Learning Locomotion with Musculoskeletal Models

RL has enabled musculoskeletal simulations to generate diverse, human-like movement [2]. Most of this progress uses motion-imitation rewards; predictive simulation works without them.

Motion Imitation [3], [4], [5]

Reward = track pre-recorded motion capture

  • reproduces reference kinematics
  • only constrains surface motion → muscles + joint kinetics underdetermined
  • brittle outside the clips (new speed, slope, task)

Predictive Simulation

Reward = task + biologically-plausible terms, no reference

  • pursued to overcome imitation’s limits
  • two complementary strategies:
    • objectives: shaping bevaior with biologically plausible objectives [6]
    • curricula: improving learning efficiency/robustness using bio-inspired curriculum design [7], [8]
  • ✗ still poor match to human joint kinetics + muscle patterns

Gap the paper is trying to address: visually plausible motion ≠ experimentally grounded biomechanics

RL has made it possible for musculoskeletal simulations to generate diverse, human-like movement. The paper splits that work by whether the reward includes motion imitation.

Most of the recent progress has come from motion imitation, where the reward is to track a recorded mocap clip. That’s great for reproducing the reference kinematics. But the paper’s point is that it only constrains the surface-level motion: the muscle coordination and joint kinetics underneath are left underdetermined, so the policy can land on internal solutions that aren’t physiologically plausible. And since it’s optimized around the demonstrations, it can be brittle when the speed, slope, or task changes.

Predictive simulation was pursued to get around that — there’s no reference motion in the reward, just the task plus biologically plausible terms. The paper describes two complementary strategies. One changes what’s rewarded: shaping behavior with biologically plausible objectives, like Schumacher et al. The other changes how training is structured: bio-inspired curricula, like Badie and Weng, which improve learning efficiency and robustness. They’re complementary because they work on different parts of the problem — the reward versus the training process.

But even predictive simulation still struggles to quantitatively match human joint kinetics and muscle patterns across speeds and slopes. So the paper frames this as a gap between motion that looks right and biomechanics that actually match experiments — and argues we need controllers that are both physiologically constrained and generalizable. That’s where synergies come in.

Muscle Synergies: Conceptually

Evidence suggests the nervous system doesn’t control each muscle separately; it activates fixed groups of muscles together [9].

Without synergies (Independent Controller)

  • the controller sets every leg muscle on its own
  • in this model: 40 muscles per leg

With synergies (Synergistic Controller)

  • turns on a group of muscles, in fixed proportions
  • the controller only decides how much of each group to use
  • in this paper: 10 muscle synergies per leg

Synergy action spaces already make RL with muscle models more sample-efficient and robust [10], [11].

I want to make sure everyone’s on the same page about what a synergy is, since the whole paper rests on it.

Normally, an RL controller for a musculoskeletal model sets every muscle on its own. In this model that’s 40 muscles per leg, so 40 knobs to turn at every time step.

The synergy idea comes from motor neuroscience: there’s evidence that the nervous system doesn’t work that way. Instead it activates groups of muscles together, and within a group the muscles always fire in the same proportions. So the controller only has to decide how much of each group to use.

In this paper, they use 10 groups per leg instead of 40 individual muscles.

Synergy action spaces aren’t new in RL — they’ve already been shown to make learning with muscle models more sample-efficient and robust. What this paper asks is whether they also make the biomechanics more human-like.

Muscle Synergies: Definition

Definition: Muscle Synergy

\[ \mathbf{a}(t) \approx \sum_{j=1}^{k} w_j(t)\,\mathbf{h}_j \]

  • \(\mathbf{h}_j\): which muscles are in group \(j\), and how strongly (fixed)
  • \(w_j(t)\): how much group \(j\) is on at time \(t\) (what the controller chooses)
  • \(k\) groups ≪ number of muscles

In the figure: \(W\) = \(\mathbf{h}_j\), \(C(t)\) = \(w_j(t)\)

Here’s the same idea more formally. The figure shows two synergies and four muscles. Each synergy, the bar plots in the middle, is a fixed recipe — the blue one uses mostly muscle 1, some of muscle 2, a little of muscle 3, and none of muscle 4. Each synergy also has an activation coefficient over time, the line plots on the outside, which says how much that group is turned on. You multiply the two together, and then add up the contributions from each synergy to get each muscle’s activity — the shaded colors at the bottom show how much of each muscle’s activity comes from each synergy.

In the equation, the h’s are the fixed recipes, and the w’s are how much each group is on over time — that’s the part the controller chooses. The figure uses different letters, W for the synergy and C for the coefficient, so I put the mapping at the bottom.

Bro

Two controllers, same everything else:

  • Synergistic: policy → 10 synergy activations per leg → \(H\) → leg muscle activations
  • Independent (baseline): policy → individual muscle excitations directly

Test: compare both to treadmill data from two open-source datasets (22 + 8 people) [13], [14]

Steps 1 through 3 are just about building the synergies. They record one person walking overground, use MocoInverse in OpenSim to estimate the muscle activations, and then run NMF on the 40 right-leg muscles to get 10 synergies — that’s the matrix H.

Step 4 is where RL comes in. Instead of choosing every leg muscle activation separately, the policy outputs 10 synergy activations per leg, and H turns those into muscle activations. The reward only has a target speed plus effort, range-of-motion, and fall terms — the recorded motion is never used as a tracking target.

To see if this helps, they train a second controller that directly controls individual muscle excitations. Then they compare both against two open-source treadmill datasets.

Data Collection

Subject: 1 healthy young male (28 y, 69.56 kg, 176 cm), no history of neuromuscular or cardiovascular disorders

Protocol: 10 trials of 10 m overground walking → 1 stride kept per trial

Data collected: optical motion capture (12 OptiTrack Prime 13 cameras, 100 Hz, 38 markers), 5 force plates (1000 Hz; 4 AMTI, 1 Bertec)

Preprocessing: 4th-order Butterworth low-pass (12 Hz) on markers + GRFs; heel strike = vertical GRF first exceeds 15 N

No EMG was collected.

The dataset is small — one participant, ten strides. The paper’s conclusion leans on this: they get human-like locomotion with minimal experimental data.

The thing to keep in mind is that they only recorded motion capture and force plates — no EMG. So we know how the person moved and the forces on their feet, but not what their muscles were doing. The next slide is how they estimate that.

Muscle Activation Estimation

  1. Build the model: H2190 (3D, 21 DoF, 90 muscles, incl. lumbar joint + 10 trunk muscles), converted from Hyfydy to OpenSim
  2. Scale: fit the model to the participant’s body size (markers from one walking trial)
  3. Inverse kinematics: marker positions → joint angles \(\mathbf{q}(t)\)
  4. MocoInverse [15]: joint angles + GRFs → estimated activations for all 90 muscles

At this point we know how the person moved and the ground reaction forces, but we didn’t measure what the muscles were doing. And because there are way more muscles than joints, lots of different muscle patterns could produce that exact same motion.

So they use an optimization to pick one. First they take the H2190 model — 3D, 21 degrees of freedom, 90 muscles — which was originally built for Hyfydy and converted to OpenSim. They scale it to the participant’s body using the markers from one walking trial, and run inverse kinematics to get joint angles.

Then MocoInverse solves an optimal control problem: find the muscle controls that reproduce the measured joint angles and ground reaction forces, while minimizing the sum of squared muscle controls. It also includes reserve and residual actuator efforts. In general OpenSim terms, these are small extra torques and forces that make up the difference if the muscles and the model can’t exactly reproduce the data — they’re penalized so the muscles do as much of the work as possible.

The output is estimated activations for all 90 muscles. The 40 right-leg muscles are what they use to extract synergies next.

The thing to keep in mind is that this is an estimate from an optimization, not EMG — and the “least effort” assumption is baked into the muscle patterns the synergies come from.

Identifying Muscle Synergies

Factor the estimated right-leg muscle activations into a small set of synergies.

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) on the 40 right-leg muscles, \(k = 10\) synergies:

\[ \underbrace{M}_{\text{activation matrix}} \;\approx\; \underbrace{W}_{\text{temporal activations}} \; \underbrace{H}_{\text{spatial weights}} \]

  • \(M\) (\(T \times 40\)) → estimated activation of each muscle over time
  • \(W\) (\(T \times 10\)) → how much each synergy is on over time; the policy produces these during RL
  • \(H\) (\(10 \times 40\)) → which muscles are in each synergy; fixed and embedded in the controller

Now they take the estimated activations for the 40 right-leg muscles and run NMF. NMF splits the right-leg activation matrix into W, which is how much each synergy is active over time, and H, which is which muscles go together and in what proportions. H is what gets embedded in the controller. The time-varying part is what the policy produces at each step.

One thing I noticed: the paper doesn’t say how they picked k = 10 — there’s no variance-accounted-for criterion or anything like that reported.

Training a Policy with RL

Two controllers are trained with the same setup — they differ only in what the policy outputs.

  • Synergistic controller (30 actions): 10 right-leg synergy activations + 10 left-leg synergy activations + 10 torso/pelvis muscle activations → fixed synergy matrix \(H\) turns them into leg muscle activations
  • Independent controller (baseline): sets every muscle excitation (80) directly

Same for both:

  • Observations: muscle states, joint angles + velocities, head motion, foot positions, GRFs, COM velocity, target speed
  • Environment: Hyfydy [16], random uneven terrain (\(-6^\circ\) to \(+6^\circ\)), target speed swept \(0.7 \to 1.6 \to 0.7\) m/s
  • Algorithm: SAC [17], 75M steps × 5 seeds

Here’s the RL setup. They train two controllers that are identical except for the action space. The synergistic policy outputs 30 numbers at each step: 10 synergy activations for each leg, plus 10 independent activations for the torso and pelvis muscles. Each leg’s 40 muscle activations come from multiplying the synergy activations through H. The independent controller is the baseline — it sets each muscle excitation directly, with no synergy structure. They also mirror activation patterns from one side to the other at heel strike to keep the gait coordinated and symmetric.

The observations are pretty standard: muscle fiber lengths and velocities, forces, excitations, and activations; joint angles and velocities; head orientation and angular velocity; foot positions relative to the pelvis; ground reaction forces; center of mass velocity; and the current target speed.

Training is in Hyfydy with the H2190 model, at 40 Hz with 25-second episodes. The terrain is 10,000 randomly generated tile surfaces with slopes from −6 to +6 degrees, and the target speed steps from 0.7 up to 1.6 and back down across episodes.

They use SAC from Stable-Baselines3 — 75 million steps per seed on an RTX 4090, with 5 seeds shared between both controllers. For the biomechanical analysis they keep the policy with the highest evaluation return, to characterize the best each controller can do.

Reward Design

Same reward for both controllers: walk at the target speed, without wasting effort, straining joints, or falling.

\[ R = w_{\text{vel}} R_{\text{vel}} + w_{\text{effort}} R_{\text{effort}} + w_{\text{rom}} R_{\text{rom}} + w_{\text{fall}} R_{\text{fall}} \]

  1. Velocity: walk at the target speed, without drifting sideways or shaking the head
  2. Effort: penalize total muscle activation
  3. Range of motion: penalize knee hyperextension + extreme lumbar bending
  4. Fall: penalize falling

No imitation term → the human data is never used as a target.

The reward has four parts, and both controllers get exactly the same one.

The velocity term is a plateaued Gaussian: full reward within 0.05 m/s of the target speed, dropping off outside that. It’s multiplied by Gaussian penalties on sideways velocity and on head angular velocity along each axis, so the model walks straight and keeps its head steady.

The effort term is the sum of squared muscle activations, with a negative weight. The range-of-motion term penalizes knee hyperextension on both legs and lumbar extension outside −20 to 10 degrees. And the fall term is 1 when an episode ends in a fall.

The main thing is what’s missing: there’s no imitation term. Nothing in the reward says “look like this person.” So the differences we’ll see between the two controllers come from the action space.

Evaluation Protocol

Compare both controllers’ gait to human treadmill data, relative to how much humans differ from each other.

  • Data: two open-source treadmill datasets, not the data used to build the synergies
  • Speed: level walking, 0.7–1.8 m/s, 22 subjects [13]
  • Slope: \(\pm 5^\circ\) at 1.2 m/s, 8 subjects [14]
  • Metrics: sagittal joint angles, joint moments, GRFs (AP + vertical), EMG for 8 lower-limb muscles

How they score it

  • RMSE ratio: sim-vs-human error ÷ average human-vs-human error in the same dataset
    • ≈ 1.0 → the sim is as far from the data as one person is from another
    • computed per variable and gait phase → count how many exceed 2, 3, and 5
  • EMG: correlation with human EMG, compared to the spread of correlations between humans

For each controller they run 10 rollouts, take the last 10 strides of each, and normalize to the gait cycle. Then they compare against two public treadmill datasets — one for speed, one for slope. Importantly, this isn’t the data they collected: the one participant’s overground walking was only used to build the synergies, so the evaluation is on completely different people, on a treadmill.

I like the RMSE ratio a lot. Instead of just reporting raw error, they divide by how different humans are from each other. So a ratio of 1 means the simulation is about as close to a person as another person would be. They also report Pearson correlations between waveforms and compare those to the spread of correlations between humans.

Results

RL Performance

These are the training curves averaged over five seeds, with the standard deviation shaded. Both controllers train stably on uneven terrain with the same speed curriculum. The independent controller rises earlier and converges to a higher reward, and that’s consistent across seeds.

But as we’ll see on the next few slides, the higher reward doesn’t translate into a more human-like gait.

Similarity of Muscle Activities

1.2 m/s level walking, 8 muscles vs. EMG (22 people)

Synergistic → within the human range for all 8

Independent → outside it for 5 / 8; GAS, ST, BF negatively correlated

For the independent controller, five of the eight muscles fall outside the range of correlations you see between humans, and GAS, ST, and BF are actually negatively correlated with the EMG.

The synergistic controller is within the human range for all eight. The plantarflexors agree in both timing and magnitude. ST, BF, and RF have lower correlations, but the paper points out those muscles also have the broadest variability between people.

One thing worth noting from the figure: for TA and RF, the independent controller’s median correlation is actually higher than the synergistic one, even though both are within the human range.

Both walk stably 0.7 → 1.8 m/s

Synergistic → keeps human speed trends (red arrows)

Independent → reverses or flattens them (green arrows): hip + knee angle in swing, knee angle + ankle moment in loading response

Both controllers walk stably across the full speed range.

In the human data, joint angles and moments change monotonically with speed in specific gait phases. The synergistic controller mostly keeps those trends — the red arrows. The independent controller has several places where the trend is reversed or flattened — the green arrows — most clearly in hip angle during swing, knee angle in loading response and swing, and ankle moment in loading response.

The worst one is the independent controller’s knee angle in swing, with an RMSE ratio of 7.98. That’s the largest kinematic deviation in the paper, and it lines up with where the speed trend is reversed.

Kinematic and Kinetic Similarity: Slope

Uphill, level, downhill (\(\pm 5^\circ\)) at 1.2 m/s

Both → reproduce the basic GRF shape

Independent → knee moment in loading response hits RMSE ratio 8.56 (~\(2\times\) synergistic’s worst)

On slopes, both controllers get the basic shape of the ground reaction forces — braking to propulsion in the AP direction, and the double peak in the vertical.

Where they differ is the joint moments. Under the slope conditions, the independent controller’s knee moment in loading response hits an RMSE ratio of 8.56, which is close to double the synergistic controller’s largest joint moment error.

Quantitative Summary: RMSE Ratios

Ratio > 3: 23 independent vs. 7 synergistic

Ratio > 5: 7 independent vs. 0 synergistic

Synergistic worst moment: hip flexion, loading response (4.16 / 4.54)

Both controllers have quite a few measures above an RMSE ratio of 2 — 39 for independent and 31 for synergistic — so neither is perfect. The difference shows up at higher thresholds: above 3, it’s 23 versus 7, and above 5, it’s 7 versus zero.

The synergistic controller has a bigger global offset in hip angle, which gives it moderately higher RMSE ratios there. Its largest kinetic error is hip flexion moment in loading response. But it avoids the extreme knee, ankle, and GRF errors the independent controller has — for example, the independent controller’s AP GRF in swing hits 7.19 under the speed conditions because of delayed toe-off.

Discussion

Limitations

  • level walking + modest slopes only (running, turning, stairs untested)

  • synergies from inverse simulation, not EMG

  • synergy set + dimensionality fixed across tasks and speeds

  • single participant used for simulation

  • biomechanical analysis uses only the best of 5 seeds

  • no criterion reported for choosing \(k = 10\)

The authors list four limitations: only walking on level ground and modest slopes, synergies from inverse simulation instead of EMG, a fixed synergy set and dimensionality, and a single participant. They do point out that even with one person’s synergies, fidelity improved across speeds and slopes.

A few things I’d add. The biggest one for me is the comparison itself: the synergistic controller’s action space is 30-dimensional, while the independent one controls every muscle. The paper itself says synergies reduce the effective action dimensionality. So is the improvement because the synergies are physiological, or just because the action space is smaller? A random or PCA basis of the same size would answer that.

The training curves show all five seeds, but the biomechanical analysis only uses the best one, so we don’t know how consistent the gait results are across seeds. They also don’t say how k = 10 was chosen. Since MocoInverse minimizes squared controls, H is probably carrying some of that optimization prior too. And the synergistic controller gets a lower reward, so the fidelity does cost some task performance.

Conclusion

Takeaways

Synergies as the action space -> physiology-informed prior from minimal data (

With only a high-level velocity target (no imitation reward):

  • muscle activation timing within cross-human variability for all 8 muscles at 1.2 m/s
  • largely preserves speed- and slope-dependent trends in human data
  • no extreme (RMSE ratio > 5) kinematic or kinetic errors

The paper’s main takeaway is that you can get much more human-like biomechanics just by constraining the action space to synergies, with very little data and only a velocity target.

The quote at the bottom is from their conclusion — the idea is to use not just a human-like body, but also the control strategy from motor neuroscience.

My own takeaway: the more human-like controller gets a lower reward, so reward alone doesn’t tell us whether the gait is realistic.
