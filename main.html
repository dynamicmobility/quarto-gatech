Muscle Synergy Priors Enhance Biomechanical Fidelity in Predictive Musculoskeletal Locomotion Simulation

Literature Review, FACTS Lab + Dynamo Lab

Ayah Ahmad

Georgia Institute of Technology

October 29, 2026

Motivation

Why learn locomotion policies for musculoskeletal models (MSMs)?

  • Enables a simulation-based study of human gait, and how it physiologically changes due to pathology.
  • Easier-to-collect data for training and personalizing assistive device controllers.

Assistive devices, like exoskeletons, can significantly increase functional mobility, especially in older age [1].

msm with exo video

The paper starts from the clinical side: walking is central to independence, and losing mobility — from aging or disease — is tied to injury and lower well-being. So we want simulations that can tell us something real about how people walk.

RL has gotten us a long way there. MSMs can now produce pretty diverse, human-like movement, and a lot of that progress has come from motion imitation.

The issue they point out is that imitation policies are really just copying the reference kinematics. They don’t necessarily capture how the muscles are coordinated or what the joint kinetics look like, and they tend to break when you move outside the demonstrations.

So their question is whether adding neurophysiological structure — muscle synergies — to the RL controller makes the simulated gait more biomechanically faithful, not just better looking.

Background

Learning Locomotion with MSMs

Train a policy using reinforcement learning to output muscle activations that achieve high-level locomotion skills, like walking, incline-walking, running, etcetera.

Motion Imitation [2], [3], [4]

  • Motion imitation reward.
  • Reproduces reference kinematics well.
  • Often uses physiological implausible muscle activations.

Predictive Simulation [5], [6], [7] There are two main approaches:

  1. Utilize bio-plausible objectives (effort, stability, pain).
  2. Build bio-inspired curricula (balance, walk, run).

Neither approach connects kinematic accuracy with neuromuscular authenticity

  • Human-like \(\neq\) physiologically plausible: the underlying neuromuscular coordination and joint kinetics are underdetermined.

Most of the recent progress in MSM locomotion has come from motion imitation. You give the policy a mocap clip and reward it for tracking it, and the kinematics come out looking great.

But matching joint angles doesn’t mean the muscles are doing anything reasonable. Each muscle is controlled independently, so the policy can do things like co-contract within a muscle group in ways no person would. The kinetics and muscle coordination are basically unconstrained. These policies also tend to be brittle — move them to a speed or slope that wasn’t in the demo and they fall apart.

Predictive simulation drops the reference trajectory entirely and lets the gait emerge from things like effort and stability objectives. The motion looks plausible, but when you actually compare against human kinetics and EMG across speeds and slopes, the agreement still isn’t good. That’s the gap this paper is going after.

Muscle Synergies

Evidence suggests that the central nervous system recruits groups of muscles together, rather than controlling each one individually [8], [9].

Definition: Muscle Synergy (spatial)

A fixed, non-negative weighting \(\mathbf{h}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{m}_{\ge 0}\) over \(m\) muscles. Activations are a time-varying combination of \(k \ll m\) synergies: \[ \mathbf{a}(t) \approx \sum_{j=1}^{k} w_j(t)\,\mathbf{h}_j, \qquad w_j(t) \ge 0 \]

  • high-dimensional muscle control → many kinematically acceptable but physiologically implausible solutions
  • ~4–6 synergies per leg reproduce walking EMG
  • clinically meaningful → paretic limb shows fewer, merged modules after stroke [10]
  • already used in over-actuated RL for sample efficiency [11], [12]

The idea behind muscle synergies is that the nervous system doesn’t drive 40 leg muscles one at a time. It recruits groups of muscles that fire together with fixed relative weights, and then just modulates how much of each group to use over time. In walking EMG, around 4 to 6 of these per leg explain most of what’s going on.

This also matters clinically — after stroke, the paretic leg tends to have fewer synergies, and they look like merged versions of the healthy ones.

People have already used synergies as an action space in RL, but mostly because it makes learning faster. This is where the paper brings in the redundancy problem: with that many muscles, there are lots of solutions that get the kinematics right but aren’t physiologically plausible. Synergies are a way to cut that space down to something closer to what the nervous system actually does.

What hasn’t really been tested is whether it also makes the resulting biomechanics more human-like, which is what this paper does.

1. Record real-world walking.

  • Extract 10 strides from clip.

\(\rightarrow\) Task-space markers & ground reaction forces (GRF).

→

2. Estimate muscle activity

→

Bro

Two controllers, same everything else:

  • Synergistic: policy → 10 synergy weights per leg → \(H\) → 40 muscles
  • Independent (baseline): policy → every muscle directly

Test: compare both to treadmill data from 30 other people (22 + 8) [14], [15]

Steps 1 through 3 are just about building the synergies. They record one person walking, use a simulation (MocoInverse in OpenSim) to estimate what their muscles were doing, since there’s no EMG, and then use NMF to find groups of muscles that tend to activate together. That gives 10 synergies per leg — that’s the matrix H.

Step 4 is where RL comes in. Instead of the policy choosing 40 separate muscle activations per leg, it chooses how much of each of the 10 synergies to use, and H turns that into muscle activations. The reward only says “walk at this speed” — the human data is never used as a tracking target.

To see if this helps, they train a second controller that’s identical except it sets every muscle directly. Then they compare both against treadmill data from 30 people who weren’t used to build anything.

Data Collection

Subject: 1 healthy young male (28 y, 69.6 kg, 176 cm)

Protocol: 10 trials of 10 m overground walking → 1 stride kept per trial

Data collected: optical motion capture (12 OptiTrack cameras, 100 Hz, 38 markers), 5 force plates (1000 Hz; 4 AMTI, 1 Bertec)

Preprocessing: 4th-order Butterworth low-pass (12 Hz); heel strike = vertical GRF > 15 N

No EMG was recorded — synergies come from an optimization, not measured muscle signals.

The dataset is small — one participant, ten strides. That’s kind of the point though, they want to show you don’t need much data to build the prior.

The thing to keep in mind for the rest of the talk is that there’s no EMG here. The muscle activity is estimated from kinematics and ground reaction forces, which I’ll show on the next slide. So the synergies are coming out of an optimization, not from actual muscle recordings.

Identifying Muscle Synergies

Estimate muscle activity with inverse simulation, then factor it with NMF.

Muscle activity estimation

  • H2190 model → 3D, 21 DoF, 90 muscles (incl. lumbar + 10 trunk muscles), ported Hyfydy → OpenSim
  • scale → IK → MocoInverse [16]: minimize \(\sum u_i^2\) s.t. measured kinematics + GRFs

Synergy acquisition — NMF on right-leg activations

\[ \underbrace{M}_{T \times 40} \;\approx\; \underbrace{W}_{T \times k}\;\underbrace{H}_{k \times 40}, \qquad W, H \ge 0, \quad k = 10 \]

  • \(W\) → temporal coefficients, discarded (policy produces these online)
  • \(H\) → spatial weights, the fixed prior in the controller

They scale the H2190 model to the subject, run inverse kinematics, and then use MocoInverse to find the muscle activations that reproduce the measured motion and forces while minimizing squared activation.

Then NMF splits that activation matrix into W, which is how much each synergy is active over time, and H, which is the actual synergy structure — which muscles go together and in what proportions. They throw W away, since that’s what the policy is going to learn to produce. H is what gets baked into the controller.

One thing I noticed: they pick k = 10 without reporting a VAF criterion, and 10 is on the high side compared to the 4 to 6 you usually see in EMG studies.

Training a Policy with RL

Action space (30-D)

  • 10 synergy weights → right leg
  • 10 synergy weights → left leg
  • 10 independent torso/pelvis activations
  • \(\mathbf{a}_{\text{leg}} = H^\top \mathbf{s}_{\text{leg}} \in \mathbb{R}^{40}\)
  • left/right mirroring at heel strike

Observations

  • muscle fiber lengths/velocities, forces, excitations, activations
  • joint angles/velocities, head orientation + angular velocity
  • feet relative to pelvis, GRFs, COM velocity, target speed

Environment: Hyfydy [17]

  • 40 Hz control, 1000-step (25 s) episodes
  • speed curriculum: \(0.7 \to 1.6 \to 0.7\) m/s
  • 10,000 random tile terrains, slopes \(\pm 6^\circ\)

Learner: SAC [18], Stable-Baselines3

  • 75M steps/seed, 5 seeds, RTX 4090
  • MLP (512-512-256), buffer \(3 \times 10^6\)
  • best seed (by return) kept for analysis

The policy outputs 10 synergy weights per leg plus 10 independent activations for the torso and pelvis, so 30 dimensions total instead of 90. Each leg’s 40 muscle activations come from multiplying those weights through H.

Training is in Hyfydy with SAC, using a speed curriculum and randomized uneven terrain. They run 5 seeds but only analyze the best one, which I’ll come back to in the limitations.

Reward Design

No imitation term — any human-likeness has to come from the action space.

\[ R = w_{\text{vel}} R_{\text{vel}} + w_{\text{effort}} R_{\text{effort}} + w_{\text{rom}} R_{\text{rom}} + w_{\text{fall}} R_{\text{fall}} \]

  1. Velocity — \(R_{\text{vel}} = R_{\text{AP}}\, R_{\text{ML}}\, R_{\text{head}}\) (track target speed, penalize lateral drift + head angular velocity)
  2. Effort — \(\sum_i a_i^2\) (negative weight)
  3. Range of motion — penalize knee hyperextension, lumbar outside \([-20^\circ, 10^\circ]\)
  4. Fall — termination penalty

The reward is pretty standard for predictive simulation. There’s a velocity term — a plateaued Gaussian around the target speed with a plus or minus 0.05 m/s dead band, times Gaussian penalties on lateral drift and head angular velocity. Then effort, a range-of-motion penalty to keep the knee and lumbar in reasonable ranges, and a fall penalty.

The main thing is what’s missing: there’s no imitation term. Nothing in the reward says “look like this person.” So if the synergistic controller ends up more human-like, it’s because of the action space.

Evaluation Protocol

Compare simulated gait to human treadmill data, relative to how much humans differ from each other.

  • Speed: level walking, 0.7–1.8 m/s, 22 subjects [14]
  • Slope: \(\pm 5^\circ\) at 1.2 m/s, 8 subjects [15]
  • Metrics: sagittal joint angles, joint moments, GRFs (AP + vertical), EMG for 8 lower-limb muscles

Definition: RMSE Ratio

\[ \text{RMSE ratio} = \frac{\text{RMSE}(\text{sim}, \text{human})} {\overline{\text{RMSE}}(\text{human}_i, \text{human}_j)} \] ratio ≈ 1.0 → the sim is as close to the data as one person is to another

For each controller they run 10 rollouts, take the last 10 strides of each, and normalize to the gait cycle. Then they compare against two public treadmill datasets — one for speed, one for slope.

I like the RMSE ratio a lot. Instead of just reporting raw error, they divide by how different humans are from each other. So a ratio of 1 means the simulation is about as close to a person as another person would be. They also report Pearson correlations between waveforms and compare those to the spread of correlations between humans.

Results

RL Performance

The independent controller gets a higher return — but that doesn’t mean it walks more like a human.

Both controllers train stably on uneven terrain with the same curriculum. The independent controller learns faster and ends up with a higher return, which makes sense since it has more freedom.

But as we’ll see on the next few slides, a higher return here doesn’t translate into a more human-like gait.

Similarity of Muscle Activities

1.2 m/s level walking → 8 muscles vs. EMG from 22 subjects

Independent

  • outside human variability for 5 / 8 muscles
  • often negatively correlated (GAS, ST, BF)

Synergistic

  • within human variability for all 8
  • plantarflexors (SOL, GAS) → timing + magnitude match
  • TA still low (\(r \approx 0.2\))

This is the result I find most convincing. For the independent controller, five of the eight muscles fall outside the range of correlations you see between humans, and several are actually negatively correlated — GAS, ST, and BF are firing at basically the wrong time.

The synergistic controller is within the human range for all eight. The plantarflexors look especially good. ST, BF, and RF are weaker, but those are also the muscles that vary most between people. The one caveat is TA, which is still only around r = 0.2.

Kinematic and Kinetic Similarity: Speed

Both walk stably 0.7 → 1.8 m/s (past the 1.6 m/s training max)

Synergistic → keeps human speed trends (red arrows)

Independent → reverses or flattens them (green arrows)

  • hip angle in swing
  • knee angle in loading response + swing
  • ankle moment in loading response

Both controllers can walk across the full speed range, including speeds faster than anything they saw in training.

In the human data, certain gait phases change monotonically with speed — for example, more hip flexion in swing as you walk faster. The synergistic controller keeps those trends, marked with the red arrows. The independent controller either flips them or flattens them out, in the green arrows — hip angle in swing, knee angle in loading response and swing, and ankle moment in loading response.

Kinematic and Kinetic Similarity: Slope

Uphill (\(+5^\circ\)), level, downhill (\(-5^\circ\)) at 1.2 m/s

Both reproduce basic GRF structure

  • AP → braking then propulsion
  • vertical → double peak

Independent → largest error is knee moment in loading response (RMSE ratio 8.56, ~\(2\times\) synergistic’s worst)

On slopes, both controllers get the overall shape of the ground reaction forces right — braking then propulsion in the AP direction, and the double peak in the vertical.

Where they differ is in the joint moments. The independent controller’s worst error is the knee moment in loading response, with an RMSE ratio of 8.56. That’s about twice as bad as the synergistic controller’s worst moment error.

Quantitative Summary: RMSE Ratios

Ratio > 3.0: 23 independent vs. 7 synergistic

Ratio > 5.0: 7 independent vs. 0 synergistic

Synergistic worst case: hip moment in loading response (4.16 / 4.54)

Putting it all together: the independent controller has 23 measures with an RMSE ratio above 3, versus 7 for the synergistic controller. Above 5, it’s 7 versus zero.

The synergistic controller isn’t perfect — it has a bigger overall offset in hip angle, and its worst case is hip moment in loading response. But it avoids the really extreme knee, ankle, and GRF errors that the independent controller has.

Limitations

Stated by the Authors

  • level walking + modest slopes only (no running, turning, stairs)
  • synergies from inverse simulation, not EMG
  • fixed synergy set + \(k\) across all conditions
  • single participant used to build \(H\)

My Concerns

  • 90-D vs. 30-D → is the gain from physiology or just lower dimensionality? (no random/PCA basis ablation)
  • only the best of 5 seeds analyzed
  • MocoInverse minimizes \(\sum u_i^2\) → \(H\) partly encodes an optimization prior
  • lower reward → fidelity has a task-performance cost

The authors are upfront about most of the obvious limitations — only walking and small slopes, synergies from simulation instead of EMG, one fixed synergy set, and one participant.

A few things I’d add. The biggest one for me is the comparison itself: the independent controller has a 90-dimensional action space and the synergistic one has 30. So is the improvement because the synergies are physiological, or just because the action space is smaller? A random or PCA basis of the same size would answer that, and it’s not in the paper.

They also only analyze the best of 5 seeds, so we don’t know how consistent the results are. And since MocoInverse minimizes squared activation, H is probably carrying some of that optimization prior too, not only neural structure. Finally, the synergistic controller gets a lower reward, so the fidelity does come at some cost to task performance.

Open Questions

  1. How can we leverage data from multiple subjects?
  2. How do we determine the optimal \(k\)?
  3. Could the synergy matrix be time-varying (over some window)?
  4. Would a patient-specific \(H\) (e.g. post-stroke, merged modules) reproduce pathological gait? [10]
  5. Does the prior transfer to assistive-device control (exoskeleton co-simulation)?

The last two are the ones I’m most interested in for my own work. If you could build H from a stroke patient — where the synergies are fewer and merged — would the policy reproduce their gait? And if so, could you use that as a training environment for exoskeleton controllers?

Conclusion

Takeaways

Muscle synergies as the action space are a cheap, physiology-informed prior → 10 strides from one person.

Without any imitation reward:

  • EMG timing within human variability for all 8 muscles
  • speed- and slope-dependent trends match human data
  • no extreme (RMSE ratio > 5) kinematic or kinetic errors

Lower RL return but higher biomechanical fidelity — reward isn’t a good proxy for human-likeness. Better controller structure may matter as much as better objectives.

The main takeaway for me is that you can get a lot more human-like biomechanics just by changing the structure of the action space, with very little data and no imitation reward.

And the fact that the more human-like controller gets a lower return says something about how we evaluate these models — reward alone isn’t telling us whether the gait is realistic.
